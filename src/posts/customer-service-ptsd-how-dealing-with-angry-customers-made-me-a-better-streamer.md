Let’s get one thing straight: I didn’t go to university to learn how to de-escalate a grieving family member who’s mad that the Wi-Fi is slow. I didn’t study for a degree to have a doctor scream at me because their call didn’t route correctly on the first try.

But here I am. A 26-year-old content creator, podcaster, and full-time extrovert who owes half of my social skills to the absolute hellscape that is institutional customer service.

If you’ve watched me stream on Twitch or Kick, you know I can handle chaos. Chat moves fast. Trolls pop up. Tech fails. Games crash. And I just keep talking. I keep laughing. I keep the energy up. People ask me all the time, "Tea, how are you so chill when everything is going wrong?"

The answer isn’t meditation. It isn’t therapy (though shoutout to my therapist, you’re doing great). The answer is that I spent years working jobs where people treated me like an NPC in their personal nightmare. And honestly? Streaming is a vacation compared to being the receptionist at an assisted living facility or the voice on the other end of a Sutter Health telecom line.

Here’s how customer service PTSD turned me into the streamer I am today.

### 1. I Learned That "No" Is a Complete Sentence (But I Still Have to Smile)

In my old jobs, you couldn’t just say no. You had to say, "I understand your frustration, but unfortunately, HIPAA regulations dictate..." while maintaining eye contact and not letting your left eye twitch.

As a receptionist at an assisted living facility, I wasn’t dealing with retail returns. I was dealing with families in crisis. People were stressed, scared, and often taking it out on the front desk because we were the only ones they could see. I had to be the calm in their storm. I had to enforce rules about visitation, privacy, and care without sounding like a robot.

On stream, it’s similar. I have mods. I have rules. But there are always people pushing boundaries. Someone asks for money. Someone demands I play a game I hate. Someone tries to start drama in chat.

Because of my background, I don’t get rattled. I don’t get angry. I just deploy the "Professional Voice." It’s calm. It’s firm. It’s polite but distant.

*"Hey, we don’t do that here. Please respect the rules."*

It’s not personal. It’s policy. And once you realize that most people are just testing limits, not actually attacking you, it becomes easy to shut it down without losing your cool. I learned to detach my self-worth from other people’s bad moods. That’s a superpower.

### 2. I Can Talk to Anyone About Anything (Even When I Don’t Want To)

I’m an extrovert. I love people. I love being the center of attention. But even I have limits. Even I have days where I don’t want to talk.

But in telecom and tech support, you don’t get a day off from talking. You have to engage. You have to make small talk. You have to find common ground with someone who is visibly upset just because their account got locked or their call dropped.

Working IT tech support for A/V and accounts at a university taught me how to explain complex things to people who are already frustrated. I learned to translate "tech speak" into human language. I learned to listen to what they *weren’t* saying.

This trained my brain to never run out of things to say. On stream, dead air is the enemy. If the game is loading, if I’m waiting for a guest, if I’m just setting up—I’m talking. I’m filling the silence. I’m keeping the energy alive.

I can talk about the weather. I can talk about food. I can talk about my mental health. I can talk about nothing. Because I’ve had conversations with strangers who were too angry to listen, I can handle a chat that’s just vibing. It’s easy. It’s second nature.

### 3. I Developed Thick Skin (And Not Just From California Sun)

People are mean. Like, really mean. And they don’t even know you.

I’ve had doctors yell at me because the Sutter Health phone system glitched. I’ve had university professors treat me like I was invisible until their projector stopped working. I’ve had family members blame me for policies I didn’t create.

When you go through that every day for years, you build armor. You realize that most people aren’t angry at *you*. They’re angry at their life. They’re angry at their job. They’re angry at themselves. And you’re just the nearest target.

On the internet, it’s the same. Trolls aren’t mad at teaonalamb. They’re mad at their own loneliness. They’re mad at their own insecurity. So when someone types something nasty in chat, I don’t take it personally. I see it for what it is: a cry for attention from someone who doesn’t know how to ask nicely.

And honestly? I just ban them. It’s the most satisfying part of the job. In telecom, I couldn’t hang up on the rude doctor. On Twitch, I can. And I do. Freely. Happily. Without guilt.

### 4. I Learned How to Read the Room (Even Through a Screen)

In customer service, you have to read tone. You have to know when someone is about to explode. You have to know when to back off and when to push forward.

Working in assisted living reception taught me empathy on a whole new level. I saw people at their most vulnerable. I saw grief, confusion, and love. I learned to read the air in a room instantly.

On stream, I don’t have body language. But I have chat. I have emotes. I have the speed of the messages. I can feel the vibe shift.

If chat gets quiet, I know I need to bring the energy up. If chat gets chaotic, I know I need to steer the conversation. If someone is hurting, I can tell. Because I’ve spent years listening to strangers tell me their problems, I know the difference between someone venting and someone crying for help.

This skill is invaluable for a podcaster. On *Tea Talks*, I interview guests. I talk about mental health. I talk about deep, personal stuff. I know when to push for more details and when to pull back. I know when to offer advice and when to just listen. Because I’ve listened to hundreds of strangers tell me their problems, I know how to hold space for my friends too.

### 5. I Appreciate the Good Days (And The Good Chatters)

After years of dealing with the worst of humanity, the good moments stand out.

In those jobs, a simple "thank you" felt like a victory. A smile from a resident felt like a hug. A professor who actually said "please" felt like a miracle.

On stream, it’s the same. When someone donates with a sweet message. When someone shares how my content helped them. When chat vibes together and makes inside jokes. Those moments hit harder because I know what the alternative looks like.

I don’t take my community for granted. I know how rare it is to find a group of people who are genuinely kind, supportive, and fun. And I protect that space fiercely. Because I’ve been in the trenches, I know how valuable it is to have a safe place to land.

### So, Was It Worth It?

Was working minimum wage jobs, getting yelled at by medical staff, and dealing with university bureaucracy worth it?

Honestly? No. Not really. I wouldn’t wish it on anyone. It was stressful. It was draining. It contributed to my depression. It made me anxious.

But did it prepare me for this life? Absolutely.

Streaming is customer service with better pay (barely) and cooler lights. Podcasting is therapy with a microphone. Being a content creator is performing for an audience that can leave at any second.

And I’m good at it. Not because I’m talented (though I like to think I am). But because I’m resilient. Because I’ve been beaten down by the public and I still showed up. Because I learned how to smile through the pain and then laugh about it later.

So if you’re in customer service right now, hanging on by a thread: I see you. I feel you. And I promise you, one day you’ll be sitting in front of a camera, talking to thousands of people, and you’ll realize that none of them are as scary as the doctor who screamed at you because his call took three seconds to connect.

Keep your head up. Keep your voice steady. And keep the tea hot.
